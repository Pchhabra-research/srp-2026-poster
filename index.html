The short version

Anxiety and psychosis-risk symptoms are often watched alongside suicidal ideation (SI). In our sample, those links held for non-SGM youth but not for sexual and gender minority (SGM) youth. For SGM youth, childhood maltreatment told more of the story.

44% vs 20%

SGM youth reported suicidal ideation more than twice as often as non-SGM youth.

r = .68

In non-SGM youth, suicidal ideation went hand in hand with anxiety and psychosis-risk symptoms. In SGM youth, it didn't.

r = .71

In SGM youth, childhood maltreatment was strongly linked to anxiety, and also to depression (r = .55).

3 of 4

links between symptoms differed significantly depending on identity group.

How we measured things
  • DepressionBeck Depression Inventory-II (BDI-II)
  • Suicidal ideationBDI-II item 9, any score above 0
  • AnxietyBeck Anxiety Inventory (BAI)
  • Psychosis-riskProdromal Questionnaire–Brief (PQ-B)
  • MaltreatmentChildhood Trauma Questionnaire (CTQ)

Analyses: chi-square test for SI rates; Pearson correlations within each group, compared with Fisher z-tests; SGM × predictor interactions in linear regression for each association that differed by group.

Results

1. How often SI was reported

In plain words: about 1 in 5 non-SGM youth reported suicidal thoughts, compared with more than 2 in 5 SGM youth. χ²(1, N = 66) = 4.54, p = .033, OR = 3.24.

Why it matters

SGM youth already face higher suicide risk. If the warning signs we rely on work differently for them, risk can be missed exactly where it matters most.

Warning signs may not transfer

Anxiety and psychosis-risk symptoms tracked suicidal ideation in non-SGM youth but not in SGM youth. Screening that leans on these symptoms as red flags could under-detect risk in SGM youth.

Trauma history deserves a place in assessment

In SGM youth, childhood maltreatment was closely tied to anxiety and depression. Routinely asking about early adversity may give a fuller picture than symptoms alone.

Relevant to early psychosis care

Over half of the clinical high-risk participants in this sample identified as SGM (14 of 27). Psychosis-risk services may be seeing many SGM youth whose risk profiles look different.

Consistent with minority stress theory

The pattern fits the idea that identity-related stress shapes how symptoms and risk factors relate, not just how severe they are.

Models built on majority samples may not generalize

Symptom associations established in broader populations should be tested within SGM groups before being applied to them.

Identity is worth measuring routinely

Without asking about sexual orientation and gender identity, differences like these stay invisible in both research and clinical data.

These are early, cross-sectional findings from a small sample, so they point to questions for larger studies rather than changes to practice on their own.

Limitations
  • Small sample, especially the SGM group (n = 25).
  • SGM youth were unevenly spread across diagnostic groups (2 of 21 healthy controls vs 14 of 27 CHR and 9 of 18 depression).
  • Cross-sectional design, so the links are not causal.
  • Suicidal ideation came from a single self-report item.
